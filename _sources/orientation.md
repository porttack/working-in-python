# How This Course Works

Read this once, in the first week. It is short. It explains what this book is,
what you are expected to do with it, and the one rule people find surprising.

---

## What this book is

This is *Working in Python*, adapted from Allen Downey's *Think Python*. Downey
wrote almost all of it. I cut some parts, added some parts, and wrote the graded
exercises. Where I added something, it is marked.

It is a real book, not a website that feeds you one activity at a time. You are
going to read it. That is not filler before the interesting part. Reading
technical prose and getting something out of it is one of the two or three most
useful things you will learn this year, and it is a skill nobody is born with.

Each chapter is a Jupyter notebook, which means the text and the code are the
same document. You can run any example, change it, break it, and see what
happens. Do that constantly. A chapter you only read is a chapter you did not
learn.

## Three kinds of work

Do not confuse these. They are graded differently and they are for different
things.

**1. Reading the chapter, at home, before lab.** One chapter a week. Come in
having read it and having run the examples. If you arrive without having read it,
lab will be an hour of watching other people work.

**2. Downey's exercises, in class, ungraded.** Every chapter ends with exercises.
We do these in lab, together, out loud, and they are not graded. That is
deliberate. They are where you are supposed to be wrong. Getting stuck in front
of me is the single most useful thing you can do with class time, and if the
grade were attached you would stop doing it.

**3. My exercises, graded.** Separate notebooks, handed out after we finish a
chapter. These are the assessment. They assume you did tiers 1 and 2.

## The AI rule

**First semester: no AI assistants. At all.** Not for writing code, not for
explaining code, not for debugging, not for "just checking." No ChatGPT, no
Claude, no Copilot, no Gemini.

**Second semester: allowed, with attribution.** Any assisted work gets a comment
saying so.

You will notice that this book's original author disagrees with me. Downey wrote
the third edition partly to teach people to use assistants well, and there are
places where he says so. That is a reasonable position and he is a better
programmer than I am. Here is why the course does it differently anyway.

In the spring you will build a program for the AP Create Performance Task and
then explain how it works, in writing and on video, without help. That is the
whole assessment. You cannot explain code you did not write, and you cannot get
good at explaining code by watching something else produce it.

More to the point: learning to program involves a specific miserable experience.
You have a program that does not work, you have no idea why, and you have to
reason your way to the cause with nothing but the error message and your own
head. That experience is not an obstacle on the way to learning. It *is* the
learning. An assistant takes it away from you, and it feels great, and you get
nothing.

In January the policy flips, because by then you will know enough to judge
whether what an assistant hands you is any good. Judging it is the real
professional skill. It sits on top of the other one. It does not replace it.

If you think this is wrong, say so in class. I would rather argue about it than
have you quietly work around it.

## What we cover, and when

**Fall, chapters 1 through 11.** Roughly one chapter per week. Arithmetic,
functions, conditionals, recursion, strings, lists, dictionaries, tuples. This
is the great majority of the programming content on the AP exam.

**After the exam, chapters 12 and 13.** Text analysis and generation, files and
databases. Chapter 12 is the best explanation I know of how tools like ChatGPT
actually work, which is why it sits where the AI policy has already relaxed.

**Chapters 14 through 19, on your own if you want them.** Object-oriented
programming. Not required, not graded, unedited from the original. If you finish
the course wanting more Python, start here.

The exam also covers the internet, data representation, cybersecurity, and the
effects of computing on people. None of that is in this book. It is in the
*Little Brother* unit, and it is close to half the exam, so do not treat it as
the easy part of the year.

## When you are stuck

In this order:

1. Read the error message. Actually read it, the whole thing, including the last
   line, which is usually the one that matters.
2. Look up the vocabulary. Every chapter has a glossary. Most confusion is a
   word you skimmed.
3. Reread the section the example came from.
4. Ask a classmate. You may discuss approaches, algorithms, and debugging
   strategy with anybody. You may not copy their code. I can tell the
   difference.
5. Ask me. This is what lab is for and there is no penalty for using it.

Being stuck is not evidence that you are bad at this. Everyone who writes code is
stuck most of the time. The difference between people who program and people who
gave up is entirely a matter of what they do in minute fifteen.
